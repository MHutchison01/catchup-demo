01 · How we ran it

Discovery first, solution later

Following the course’s discovery discipline — ask for desired outcomes, not solutions — we opened with unprompted narrative questions and only introduced the availability concept at the very end. That order is what makes the evidence trustworthy.

10 interviews
Parents, shift workers, students, young professionals and friends who’ve moved away — a deliberate spread of life stages.
Q1–Q3 open & unprompted
“Walk me through a recent catch-up…” The friction pattern emerged in respondents’ own words, with no steer toward our idea.
Q4–Q5 concept probe
Only here did we ask about seeing availability and sharing it — treated as directional signal, weighted below the organic stories.
JTBD lens
Coded for the job people were hiring a catch-up to do — functional, emotional and social — not the features they asked for.

Questions 4 and 5 (“would you be more likely to make plans if you knew they were free?” / “would you share your availability?”) are closed, leading and solution-adjacent — they describe Catch Up’s mechanism, then ask people to agree. Nearly everyone said yes, which is unsurprising and weak as validation. Our strong evidence is the unprompted narrative in Q1–Q3, where the same friction surfaced with no prompt toward our solution. We flag this openly rather than counting the “yes”es as proof.

02 · The core finding

Visibility is necessary. It isn’t the whole product.

Nine of ten described the same failure: plans that stall not for lack of wanting, but because organising is heavy and asking is risky. Two forces, not one, keep willing friends apart — and only one of them is an information problem.

The job, in the user’s words
“When I finally have some free time and realise I miss my people, help me find who’s genuinely free and lock in a catch-up — without the effort of chasing, or the sting of asking and getting a no.”
1The effort barrier

For the busiest respondents the bottleneck is admin, not knowledge. They know their friends exist; they can’t face the organising. Visibility they’d have to go and check is just more work.

“It’s not that I don’t know when people are free, it’s that even the organising of the knowing is too much effort.” — Shift worker, 40
2The rejection risk

For students and young professionals the block is emotional. A couple of no’s and initiation shuts down — people “pattern-recognise” rejection and build a story before they’ve even asked.

“It’s very easy to be demotivated to ask the person again if you’ve been turned down once.” — MBA student

05 · Kano analysis

Which features earn love, and which just avoid anger

The research sorts cleanly onto the Kano model. Some things are invisible until they’re missing (privacy, freshness); some scale with quality; a few are genuine delighters that no incumbent offers — and two would actively hurt us if we shipped them.

Must-be
Absent = churn. Present = merely tolerated. The price of entry.
  • Friends-only, granular privacy control
  • Accurate free / busy (never event names)
  • Stays fresh on its own — no manual upkeep
Performance
More is better. Satisfaction scales linearly with quality.
  • Breadth of overlap surfaced
  • Speed from “free” to locked-in plan
  • How far ahead gaps are flagged
Attractive
Unexpected delight. Nobody asked, everybody lights up. Our wedge.
  • Proactive match & one-tap nudge
  • Energy / activity-level signal
  • Reconnection triggers for dormant ties
  • Claim-the-gap window protection
Indifferent
Users don’t care either way. Not the job — don’t invest.
  • Discovering brand-new people
  • A public content feed
Reverse
Adding these makes it worse. The interviews warned us directly.
  • Public broadcast of availability
  • Over-notifying / another status to babysit
06 · Competitive landscape

Nobody owns intentional, in-person connection

We mapped how today’s tools serve the job across eight connection dimensions. Toggle a category to compare. The white space is clear: calendars are intentional but cold, messaging is where coordination happens but it’s high-effort and lossy, and nothing turns free time into a real-world catch-up.

Eight dimensions, scored 0–10. Qualitative estimates prepared by Syndicate 3 for illustration, not survey data. Catch Up scores low on “discovering new people” on purpose — that isn’t the job.

🔥 The real competitor: doing nothing
The status quo isn’t a rival app — it’s the plan that quietly fizzles. “It fizzles more than it happens.” Beating inertia is the win.
💬 Messaging & polls
WhatsApp / Messenger group chats are where coordination lives today — and where it dies. “Forty messages, everyone’s half-answered, the moment’s gone.”
📅 Shared calendars
Intentional and good at getting to in-person, but cold, effortful and built for work — not for “who’s up for a quiet one Thursday.”
07 · SWOT

Where the opportunity is strong — and where it’s fragile

A candid read of the concept against what the research told us, good and bad.

💪 Strengths
  • Problem validated unprompted in 9 of 10 interviews
  • Emotionally significant — loss language, not mild annoyance
  • Consistent across ages, life stages and cities
  • A novel, well-evidenced wedge: reducing the fear of asking
⚠️ Weaknesses
  • A passive “see everyone’s calendar” view under-serves the highest-pain users
  • Value depends on data staying fresh — decay risk
  • “Available” doesn’t mean “wants the same activity”
  • Cold-start: needs a friend group present to work
🚀 Opportunities
  • Proactive match & nudge — a layer no incumbent offers
  • Energy / activity-level signalling to cut social pressure
  • Lead-time surfacing purpose-built for parents & carers
  • Reconnection triggers for long-distance friendships
🏹 Threats
  • Second-hand exposure — mutual friends leak your availability
  • “Redundancy” risk: dies like an unmaintained Teams status
  • Incumbents (calendar, messaging) bolt on a lite version
  • Privacy fears suppress the honest sharing the model needs
08 · Vision & strategy

From evidence to a strategy kernel

The research resolves into a product vision and a Rumelt strategy kernel — a diagnosis, a guiding policy, and the coherent actions that follow — plus the things we’re deliberately saying no to.

Product vision statement
For time-poor adults whose schedules have drifted from their friends’ who miss the people they love but are worn down by organising and the fear of asking, Catch Up is a private, friends-only availability & nudge app that surfaces when your people are genuinely free and locks in a plan in one tap. Unlike group-chat polls and shared calendars, which pile the effort and the rejection risk onto you, Catch Up does the noticing for you and turns a low-stakes tap into a real plan.
1Diagnosis

Catch-ups don’t fail for lack of desire. They fail because organising is high-effort and asking carries rejection risk — so willing friends quietly drift apart. Visibility helps, but alone it’s just more admin.

2Guiding policy

Treat availability as infrastructure, not the product. Win by removing effort (proactively surface the plan) and de-risking the ask (friends-only, no counted rejections, activity-level signalling) — starting with the two highest-pain personas.

3Coherent actions

Proactive match & nudge; lead-time-aware surfacing; energy signalling; granular friends-only privacy that stays fresh by itself; reconnection triggers for dormant ties.

Explicit no’s
×a discovery / dating network×public availability broadcast×another manual status to maintain×an open calendar dump
